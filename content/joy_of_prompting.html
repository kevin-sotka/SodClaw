The argument

Calm is the counter-argument.

The fail-video guy needs you to watch a model get something wrong. That's his whole act, and it works, because being right is a good feeling and he's selling it cheap. You can't beat that by arguing with him. You beat it by sitting down, saying one short sentence, and having a nice afternoon on camera while a thing gets built.

Never name the foil

Bob Ross never mentioned another painter. Not once, in eleven years. Naming the other guy makes you the reply guy and makes him the subject.

The rebuttal is the finished thing on the desk.

Show the failures anyway

Every episode includes at least one thing going wrong, uncut. Hiding them would make the whole show a lie, and viewers can smell it.

The move isn't that it never breaks. It's that breaking costs ninety seconds.

Hide the trick, then give it away

Episodes one through four look like magic. Something is always sitting on the desk that nobody comments on.

Episode five puts it into words. From then on the trick runs in the open, which somehow plays better.

The set

Six things that repeat every week.

A show is a set of promises you keep. These are the six. They cost nothing, they're all shootable on a kitchen table, and together they are the entire brand.

01

The Palette

A hand sets one physical object beside the keyboard, and Sod names it out loud like Ross named his paints — "today's supplies: six index cards and a cup of coffee." Ten seconds, no explanation of why it's there.

The ingredient is always visible. Only the ratio stays hidden until episode five.

02

The Meter

A small corner overlay all season: PROMPT 2% / CONTEXT 98%. It's on the thumbnails too. It is the show's logo and its thesis and its spoiler, all at once.

03

The Mug

Different every episode, never acknowledged. Ranges from "WORLD'S OKAYEST PROMPT ENGINEER" to a wobbly kid-painted one. Free running gag; people screenshot these.

04

The Happy Accident count

When something goes wrong, a small tally ticks up in the corner. No sting, no zoom, no record scratch. Failure gets treated like weather.

05

The last ten seconds

Music drops out. Room tone. Sod looks at the finished thing, not the camera. Silence is the flex in a format built on noise.

06

"Muy relaxando"

Once per episode, dropped without comment, usually right after something breaks. It's already how Sod talks. Don't over-use it and don't explain it.

Format

The shape of seven minutes.

Same skeleton every week, so the surprise can live in the content instead of the structure. Runtimes flex from 5:00 to 9:00; the proportions hold.

0:00–0:30 Cold open

Black screen. One sentence types itself. Enter. No greeting, no explanation, no "hey guys."

0:30–1:00 Title

Card, then: "today we're making…" Fifteen words about the thing, not about AI.

1:00–1:45 The Palette

Overhead. A hand sets something on the desk. Beat. Moves on without a word.

1:45–5:15 The Build

One take, near real time. The waiting is not cut out. Talking over the wait is the show.

5:15–6:00 Happy accident

Something is wrong. He never argues with it. He tells it what it didn't know.

6:00–7:00 Payoff & out

The thing gets used for real, once. Then: "everything I put on the desk is in the description."

Season arc

Magic, then the turn, then the open secret.

Ten episodes is a season, not a lifestyle. It has a shape: build trust with four unexplained wins, spend it all in one six-minute reveal, then do the same trick with the curtain up. The stripe on each episode below is its phase.

Episodes 01–04

Pure magic

One sentence in, a real thing out. Nobody explains the objects on the desk. Comments start asking about the corner meter around episode three.

Episode 05

The turn

Shortest episode. No build. He tells you what he's been doing and hands you the template.

Episodes 06–09

Open secret

Same trick, narrated out loud now. The builds get more personal and more ambitious because the audience can finally follow the reasoning.

Episode 10

Handoff

He builds with a stranger's materials, gives away the whole kit, and tries to make himself unnecessary.

The curriculum

What somebody actually learns.

Ten builds is a portfolio. Ten named skills is a curriculum — and a curriculum is what turns a viewer into somebody who hires you. So every episode says its skill out loud in the last fifteen seconds and puts it on screen as plain text. One line, no framework, no acronym.

EpSkillWhat it actually is
01Bring your own nounsThe model has every verb and none of your nouns. Recognizing that your own private knowledge is the scarce input, not the phrasing.
02Hand over the fileOne CSV beats five hundred words describing it. Knowing the moment to stop typing and start attaching — the highest-leverage habit an ops person can pick up.
03Overrule it on purposeUsing it to accelerate a decision while keeping authority over the decision. The anti-slop skill.
04Transcribe, don't improvePassing along a stakeholder's actual words instead of your polished version. This is requirements gathering, and it is taught by a six-year-old.
05Write the context fileThe keystone. The only skill here that compounds — twenty ugly minutes that keep paying out for years.
06Ask what it's unsure aboutProvenance and verification. The most important AI-literacy skill there is, and almost nobody teaches it in a form anyone enjoys watching.
07Add information, don't argueTreating a wrong answer as missing context rather than a verdict. The emotional skill that decides whether someone is still using this in week three.
08Capture before you organizeGetting analog reality into a form a model can touch. No scanner, no app, no subscription — just a phone and a kitchen table.
09Say the taste out loudConverting preference into specification. The entire distance between generic output and output that sounds like a person.
10Run the method on someone else's messGeneralization. The proof that what got learned was a method and not a recipe you happened to memorize.

Ten episodes about AI. Not one of them is about prompting.

Season one.

01

Phase I · Pure magic · 6:30

One Page, Your Whole Life

A single page that shows everything you're supposed to be keeping track of. Built before the coffee goes cold.

The whole prompt

Make me a one-page dashboard for my life. I'll tell you what's on it.

Seventeen words. Typed on camera. The second half of that sentence is doing all the work and nobody notices for four episodes.

Steps

  1. Say the sentence. Hit enter. Don't add anything. Let the silence sit.
  2. It asks six questions back. Answer them out loud, like a person, not like a form.
  3. Read the six index cards into the chat, one at a time. This is the whole episode and it looks like nothing.
  4. Open the file. It's ugly. Say so out loud — "that's ugly" — because pretending otherwise costs trust.
  5. One more line: "make it look like something I'd actually open on my phone."
  6. Save to the home screen. Hold the phone up to the lens. Done.

Scenes

0:00
Black. A cursor blinking. One sentence types itself, character by character. Enter key, close-miked.
0:14
Cut to Sod in the chair. Window light, plant, mug, a keyboard that has clearly been used.
0:35
Title card on black: THE JOY OF PROMPTING · No. 1. Four seconds. No motion graphics.
1:05
Overhead, locked off. A hand sets six handwritten index cards on the desk. Beat. Hand leaves. No comment, no music cue.
1:45
Screen share, one continuous take. Sod's voice over the waiting.
5:20
The dashboard on the actual phone, held in an actual hand, in the actual room.
6:15
Outro. The corner meter is the last thing on screen before black.

Feel

Sunday morning. Nothing at stake, nobody's boss is watching. The pace of a man who has already decided this will work and isn't in a hurry to prove it.

Zero urgency in the voice. If a shot feels slow in the edit, keep it.

Music

Nylon-string guitar, fingerpicked, no click track — one chord change every four bars. A Rhodes pad slides underneath when the file first opens.

Everything drops out for the last ten seconds. Room tone only.

Script

Cold open

Okay. That's it. That's the whole prompt.
(pause, hand off the keyboard)
Let's see what it does.

At the cards — 1:05

I wrote some stuff down this morning. Nothing fancy. Just the things I keep forgetting.
(sets them down, moves on immediately)

Happy accident — 4:40

Oh — it thinks "gym" is a project.
(genuinely considering it)
Which, honestly, fair.
(types)
Gym isn't a project, it's a habit. Habits get their own row.
…There we go.

Outro — 6:15

That's your page. Put it on your home screen so you actually look at it.
Everything I set on the desk today is in the description, in case you want to be boring and read it.
Muy relaxando.

What actually did the work

Six index cards, handwritten, thirty seconds of effort. The model brought the HTML — it has written ten million dashboards. It had never heard of The Wall, or the novel, or the fact that Tuesdays are useless. That came off the cards.

Content it throws off

  • Vertical, 45s: the phone reveal, nothing else.
  • Blog: "I put my whole life on one page and now I have to look at it."
  • Post: photo of the six index cards. No caption. Let them ask.

Today's skillBring your own nouns — The model has every verb and none of your nouns. The first skill is noticing that what you already know is the scarce part.

02

Phase I · Pure magic · 8:00

The Newsletter That Writes Itself

A weekly newsletter for a fantasy league that has beef, history, and one guy who deserves it.

The whole prompt

Write my fantasy football league a weekly newsletter. Be mean to the guy in last place.

The comedy engine of the episode is that this prompt produces something genuinely terrible the first time, and it airs.

Steps

  1. The prompt. Enter. Wait.
  2. Read the first draft out loud, flatly, all the way through. It is generic mush. Do not hide it.
  3. Hand it the league export. One CSV, dragged into the window on camera.
  4. Same prompt, same model, second draft. It now has names, grudges, and a fourteen-point blowout in it.
  5. Add three sentences about who these people actually are — who's smug, who's new, who cheated in 2021.
  6. Wire it to send itself Tuesdays at 9am and never touch it again.

Scenes

0:00
The prompt typing itself on black. The word "mean" lands and the cursor pauses on it for a beat.
0:50
The bad draft, full screen. Sod reading it deadpan. No music. This is the funniest 40 seconds of the season.
2:10
Overhead: a printed CSV, folded in fourths, coffee ring on it, unfolded under a desk lamp.
2:35
The drag-and-drop. Held for the full upload. Don't cut it.
3:40
Second draft. Sod stops reading mid-sentence because he's actually laughing.
6:30
Phone: the sent email. Then the group chat lighting up, names blurred, thumb scrolling.
7:30
Outro on the empty desk, laptop closed.

Feel

A bit that turns into a real thing. Starts as a comedy episode about a bad draft and ends with a piece of software that will genuinely run for eighteen weeks.

The turn from "this is junk" to "oh no, it's good" happens on camera, unedited. That turn is the episode.

Music

Brushed drums, upright bass, a lazy walking line. A single organ stab on the roast line.

Music cuts out completely for the bad draft — comedy needs silence — and comes back mid-phrase when the CSV lands.

Script

The bad draft — 0:50

"The competition was fierce this week."
(long pause, looking at camera)
Was it, though.

The CSV — 2:35

Same model. Same prompt. I just gave it the actual league.

Second draft — 3:40

"Brandon has now started a player on a bye week three times this season, which is impressive, because there have only been four byes."
(laughs, sits back)
Okay. Okay, that's… yeah. That's going out.

Outro — 7:30

Tuesdays at nine. I don't write it.
(closes laptop)
I just get blamed for it.

What actually did the work

One CSV export. Fourteen kilobytes. That is the entire difference between a press release and a roast. Draft one had no facts, so it wrote a horoscope. Draft two had a season, so it wrote comedy.

Content it throws off

  • Post: the Brandon line, standalone, no setup.
  • Ship: Gridiron Gazette issue #1 goes out for real, same day.
  • Short: split screen, bad draft vs. good draft, no narration.

Today's skillHand over the file — One export beats five hundred words describing the export. Knowing the moment to stop typing and start attaching.

03

Phase I · Pure magic · 7:30

Too Many Projects

A tracker for the person who starts everything and finishes eighty percent of it. Ends by telling him to quit two.

The whole prompt

I have too many projects and I don't know which one to work on. Fix it.

The only prompt in the season that admits to a problem instead of requesting a thing. It gets the most comments.

Steps

  1. Prompt. Enter. Let it ask its questions instead of pre-answering them.
  2. Dump the shoebox of sticky notes on the desk. Read every single one out loud, including the embarrassing ones.
  3. Ask it to score each project two ways: how much you'd care if it shipped, and how hard it actually is.
  4. Argue with three of the scores on camera. Change them. You are the taste. It is the typing.
  5. Ask for one page that tells you what to do today. One sentence at the top.
  6. Ask the question you've been avoiding: "which of these should I kill?"

Scenes

0:00
The prompt on black. "Fix it." holds an extra beat.
1:10
The sticky dump, overhead, in slow motion. The only slow motion in the season — spend it here.
1:30
Reading them out. Cut on the funny ones. One just says "trains?"
3:15
Scoring on screen, two columns filling in.
4:20
Sod pushing back at the screen, out loud, mid-sip.
5:40
A scatter chart. Eleven dots. Two of them way off in the corner nobody wants to be in.
6:30
The final page. One sentence. Sod reads it silently, then just nods. Hold on his face for three full seconds.

Feel

The one with a lump in the throat. A person with too many ideas gets told, kindly and with evidence, to pick one.

Do not play the ending as triumphant. The ending is relief. Those are different and the audience knows the difference.

Music

Solo Rhodes, no drums, one repeated four-note figure that never resolves. Guitar enters only for the last screen.

Nothing swells. If it swells, it's a productivity video, and productivity videos are the enemy of this show.

Script

The dump — 1:30

Some of these are from 2023.
(holds one up to the lens)
This one just says "trains?" With a question mark. I don't know what past me wanted.

Arguing with a score — 4:20

No. That one's a four, not a two.
(typing)
It's hard because I keep not doing it, not because it's actually hard. That's a real difference and you should know it.
(to camera)
It'll take that. It's not precious about it.

The kill — 6:30

It says shelve two of them.
(pause)
…It's right. Which is annoying.

Outro — 7:10

The tracker isn't the point. The point is that on Sunday night, one sentence tells you what Monday is.

What actually did the work

Forty sticky notes and eleven arguments. The model never decided anything — every score that mattered got overruled on camera. What it did was make deciding fast enough to actually finish, which is a different and better trick.

Content it throws off

  • Post: the overhead sticky-dump photo, unedited.
  • Blog: "I let an AI tell me to quit two projects."
  • Download: the two-axis scoring sheet, free, no email.

Today's skillOverrule it on purpose — Let it accelerate the decision; keep the decision. Every score that mattered got overruled on camera.

Phase I · Pure magic · 9:00

A Game With a Leaderboard, Before Bedtime

A six-year-old writes the design document out loud. Forty minutes later Grandma is on the leaderboard.

The whole prompt

Make a game my kid would actually play. He likes trains and he likes winning.

Longest episode of the season, and the only one where the co-host is under four feet tall.

Steps

  1. The kid says the idea. Camera on the kid's hands and the drawing, not the screen.
  2. Type what the kid said. Word for word. Do not improve it. "There's lava" is a real specification.
  3. First playable in one prompt. Play it immediately. It's bad and the kid says why.
  4. Say what the kid said. Again, word for word. He is the product manager and everyone on camera knows it.
  5. "Add a leaderboard so he can beat me." One line, and it's a real leaderboard with real persistence.
  6. Send the link to Grandma. She plays it. She is now in third place.

Scenes

0:00
Black. A kid's voice, unaccompanied, describing the game. No visual at all for eleven seconds.
0:40
A crayon drawing being taped to the monitor bezel. It stays there for the whole episode.
1:30
Two heads at one screen, one of them bouncing.
3:20
First playtest. The kid's face is not on camera; his verdict absolutely is.
5:50
The fix, then the second playtest, and the difference is instantly visible.
7:20
The leaderboard. Three names. Dad is second.
8:20
The kid's reaction to being number one. Wide shot. Do not cut it short.

Feel

The warmest thing in the season and the easiest to over-produce. Resist. Dad energy, one camera, a kitchen table.

Never let the adult be the smart one. Every good idea in the episode came from the six-year-old and the edit should make that obvious.

Music

Toy piano and ukulele, deliberately slightly out of tune. Real drums enter only when the kid takes first place.

Then it stops dead on the high score. Hard cut to room tone and a kid yelling.

Script

Cold open — kid, over black

KID: It's a train, and you have to get to the station, but there's lava.

Typing it — 1:30

I'm not going to make that sound smarter than it is. "There's lava" is the design document.

Playtest — 3:20

KID: It's too easy.
SOD: Say that again, but tell me why.
KID: Because the lava doesn't move.
SOD: (already typing) The lava doesn't move. Copy that.

Outro — 8:20

KID: I'm number one.
SOD: You are number one.
(to camera)
Forty minutes. He'll play it for a year.

What actually did the work

A six-year-old and a crayon drawing. "The lava doesn't move" is a better bug report than most bug reports, and "he likes trains and he likes winning" is a better brief than most briefs. The spec was excellent. It just wasn't typed.

Content it throws off

  • Post: "Because the lava doesn't move." — best product feedback I've ever gotten.
  • Vertical: the high-score moment. Needs no context and travels furthest.
  • Devlog: Roblox Railways post, with the crayon drawing as the header image.

Today's skillTranscribe, don't improve — “The lava doesn't move” is a better bug report than your tidied-up version of it. This is requirements gathering, taught by a six-year-old.

Phase II · The turn · 6:00

The Happy Little Context File

No build. The shortest episode of the season, and the only one that matters. He tells you what's been on the desk.

The whole prompt

Write a paragraph about me.

Five words. The smallest prompt of the season sets up the biggest point in it.

Steps

  1. Ask it to write about you cold. Read the result out loud. It is a horoscope.
  2. Write four hundred words about yourself in a plain text file. Ugly, honest, twenty minutes. Film all twenty. Cut to four.
  3. Same prompt, same model, file attached. Read the result. It's you, and it's a little unsettling.
  4. Point at the corner meter. Say the number out loud for the first time in five weeks.
  5. Place every object from episodes 1–4 on the desk, in order, in silence.
  6. Tell them the trick isn't a trick, and give away the template.

Scenes

0:00
The desk, wide, completely empty for the first time all season. No palette object. Long hold. Regular viewers will feel it before they can name it.
0:45
The cold paragraph on screen. Read flatly, all of it.
1:30

3:20

4:05

4:40

5:30
The corner meter fills to 98% and stays up through the outro.

Feel

The magician putting the cards down on the table. Not smug, not a reveal-sting — relieved. He's been wanting to tell you since episode one.

If this episode feels like a lecture, it has failed. It should feel like a confession that turns out to be good news.

Music

Nothing until 3:40. Three minutes and forty seconds of no music in a season otherwise scored end to end.

The reveal is the episode. Four hundred words, written badly, in a file with no formatting. Everything before this was setup and everything after is the same trick performed with the curtain up — which, weirdly, plays better.

Phase III · Open secret · 9:00

Great-Great-Grandma's Paper Trail

A name, a town, and a decade. By the end there's a one-page family sheet in a relative's hands.

The whole prompt

Help me find out who my great-grandmother actually was.

First episode where the palette gets narrated out loud instead of just placed. The curtain is up now.

Steps

  1. Say the name, the town, the decade. That's all you've got and that's fine.
  2. Show it the photograph — both sides. Ask what it can read on the back.
  3. Hand it the census page and ask it to explain the columns like you're a person. This is the unlock; census forms are unreadable by design.
  4. Ask for a timeline, and then ask it what's missing from the timeline.
  5. Ask what it is not sure about. Keep that list separately. This is the ethics beat and it is not optional.
  6. Verify one claim yourself, on camera, at the actual archive. Show the checking.

Scenes

0:00
Macro on a photograph. Pencil handwriting on the back, out of focus, then sharp.
1:15

2:40

4:30
A timeline assembling itself. Gaps highlighted in a different color.
6:00
Hard cut to a real archive website in a browser. Sod checking a date himself, no AI on screen at all.
7:20
The moment it confirms. Sod, quiet, hand over his mouth.
8:20
The printed family sheet handed across a kitchen table to an older relative. No dialogue. Just their hands.

Feel

Detective work with grandma stakes. The most genuinely moving payoff of the season and also the most careful one.

This is where "go verify it" gets said out loud and meant. An episode about somebody's real ancestor that plays fast and loose would poison the whole show.

Music

Solo piano, sparse, slightly out of time — a music box somebody forgot to wind. A field recording of paper being handled sits underneath.

No drums anywhere in this episode. Nothing enters at the confirmation; let the silence do it.

Three pieces of paper that were sitting in a drawer. The model has never met your family and never will. What it's extraordinary at is reading terrible handwriting and explaining a bureaucratic form from 1910. You brought the drawer.

Phase III · Open secret · 7:00

Happy Accidents

Four things go wrong on purpose, uncut, on camera. Total elapsed time: eleven minutes. There is still a plant tracker at the end.

The whole prompt

Build me something that keeps my houseplants alive.

The stakes are deliberately trivial, because the episode is not about plants. It's the season's only answer to the fail-video genre and it's delivered in one sentence at 5:40.

Steps

  1. Prompt. Get something wrong. React with roughly the energy of noticing it's raining.
  2. Don't argue. Add information. Put the rule on screen as a title card and leave it there.
  3. Get it wrong again. Add information again. Do not speed this up in the edit.
  4. The fourth one is real and cannot be talked out of. Show that too. Solve it a different way — by hand.
  5. Put the numbers on screen: four failures, eleven minutes, one working thing.
  6. Say the thing about failure. Once. Without naming anybody.

Scenes

0:00
The prompt. Then, immediately, a wrong answer. No build-up at all.
0:30
Corner counter appears: HAPPY ACCIDENTS: 1. It will tick to 4.
1:10
Title card, plain: "I never tell it it's wrong. I tell it what it didn't know."
3:40
Failure three. Same reaction as failure one. The sameness is the joke.
4:50
Failure four. Sod stops, thinks, and opens a text editor to do it manually.
5:40
Split screen: elapsed clock at 11:04, failure counter at 4, finished tracker on the right.
6:30
Watering an actual plant, with the tracker open on the phone on the windowsill.

Feel

Deliberately anticlimactic. The stakes never rise. Nothing escalates. That flatness is the argument, and it only works if you have the nerve to leave it flat.

No sting sounds, no zoom-ins on errors, no "uh oh." Failure is weather.

Music

One eight-bar loop that restarts from the top on every failure and never changes key, tempo, or intensity.

By the fourth restart it's funny, and the joke is entirely that the music refuses to escalate along with the situation.

Script

Failure one — 0:30

Yep. That's wrong.
(types, unbothered)
It doesn't know what a pothos is. Neither did I in 2019.

Failure four — 4:50

This one I can't talk my way out of.
(opens an editor)

The Shoebox

Six years of paper on a shelf becomes a thing you can search in two seconds. The box goes in the recycling on camera.

The whole prompt

I have a box of paper and I don't want a box of paper.

The most useful episode of the season for a normal human, and it knows it. Zero software purchased.

Steps

  1. Photograph everything. Phone, kitchen table, half an hour. No scanner, no app, no subscription.
  2. Ask it to sort the pile without telling it your categories. See what it thinks your paper is.
  3. Fix its categories. Yours are better, because you live here.
  4. Ask for one searchable file. Not eleven files. One.
  5. Ask the question you could never answer before: "when does the dishwasher warranty end?"
  6. Recycle the box. On camera. It's cathartic and it's the shot.

Domestic and unglamorous on purpose. No part of this looks like technology. It looks like a Saturday chore that finally got finished.

This is the episode people send to their parents, so keep every step doable by somebody who owns exactly one device.

A metronomic, faintly ridiculous marimba pattern for the photographing montage — the sound of a boring job going fast.

The Bedtime Story Machine

A raccoon, a gas station, and a lost hat. Ends with a printed book and a lamp going off.

The whole prompt

Tell my kid a story. He'll tell you what it's about.

The palette here is the one that surprises people: three book titles and a sentence about why each one matters. Taste, said out loud, is a data format.

Steps

  1. The kid gives three ingredients: a character, a place, a problem. Nothing more.
  2. Pull the three books he actually makes you read. Read the titles in — plus one sentence each on what he loves about them.
  3. Get a story. Read it out loud on the couch. He will interrupt. Good.
  4. Change it live, mid-story, on his note. Do not defend the draft.
  5. Make it a real printed book with his drawing on the cover.
  6. Read it at actual bedtime. End the episode on the lamp.

The one you send to your mom. Softest thing in the season, sitting right before the handoff so the finale lands on warmth instead of tech.

The bench

Fourteen more, for when season one works.

Same format, same one-sentence contract, same object on the desk. These are ordered roughly by how confident I am they'd land — the first four are the ones I'd shoot without thinking about it. Do not start any of these until episode ten is published.

The Recipe Box

Turn my grandmother's recipe cards into a cookbook.

Palette: her actual index cards, in her handwriting. Rhymes with episode one and detonates emotionally. The handwriting-reading is the magic trick and the smell of the box is the story.

Read the Lease

Explain what I signed and tell me what I should be scared of.

Palette: a PDF and a handwritten list of your actual worries. Enormous reach — everyone has signed something they didn't read. Needs a clear "this isn't legal advice" beat, played straight.

Sunday Money

Tell me where my money actually goes. Don't be nice about it.

Palette: twelve months of bank exports. The budget you'll actually open, because you didn't build it — and the roast format from episode two, pointed inward.

Translate Grandpa

There's a tape of my grandfather and I don't speak the language.

Palette: one audio file. Transcript, translation, and then the part nobody expects — asking what the idioms actually meant. Highest emotional ceiling on the whole bench.

The Garage

Tell me what's in my garage and what it's worth.

Palette: eighty phone photos, same technique as the shoebox. Ends with an actual listing posted and an actual thing sold.

Coach's Clipboard

I volunteered to coach and I have no idea what I'm doing.

Palette: a roster and the parents' constraint texts. Every rec league in America needs this and the parent-volunteer audience is enormous and underserved.

Your Town in 1950

What was on this corner before it was a Chipotle?

Palette: a library archive login and one street address. Feeds Train Lore directly and it's endlessly repeatable in any town.

The Group Chat Yearbook

Make a book out of a year of this group chat.

Palette: one export, and a paragraph on who these people are to you. The privacy conversation is part of the episode, not an asterisk.

The Job Application

Help me apply for this without lying.

Palette: a real posting and an honest list of what you've actually done. Massive practical reach; "without lying" is the whole positioning and it should stay in the title.

The Doctor Folder

Organize my mom's medical paperwork so I can find things.

Palette: a folder of after-visit summaries. Extremely high value, needs the most careful handling of anything on this list — organize and explain, never advise. Say that on camera.

Nobody Reads the Newsletter

Look at my numbers and tell me what to stop doing.

Palette: your own analytics export, warts included. The honest-metrics episode. Publishing your real bad numbers buys more credibility than any win.

The Second Brain That Isn't

Palette: the note export, and the delete key. The contrarian episode: the tool was never the problem. Ends with deleting the system on camera.

The Band Rider

Make my weird hobby look like a real thing.

Palette: a set list, three gig photos, one paragraph about what you sound like. Hobby-to-small-business, and Sod can actually play, which makes it real.

Making it & getting it seen

The rules that keep this cheap and consistent.

Ten episodes on nights and weekends only survives if the format decides most things in advance. These are the decisions made once, so they never get made again at 9pm on a Tuesday.

Never say "how to" in a title

Not once, all season. "One Page, Your Whole Life," not "How to Build a Dashboard With AI."

Tutorial titles get tutorial viewers, who leave. Story titles get an audience.

The thumbnail is the palette object

Photographed on black, with the prompt in Courier underneath and the 2%/98% bar in the corner. That's the template, forever.

No face, no arrows, no open mouth, no red circles. The restraint is itself the counter-positioning — it looks like nothing else in the category.

The description is the deliverable

Every episode's description contains the actual files. The index cards, the CSV headers, the template, the prompt verbatim.

Free and ungated all season, with exactly one exception: the episode five template. Give away the method, keep the list.

Answer comments with a prompt

Someone asks how to adapt it — reply with the sentence they should type and the thing they should put on their desk. Two lines, no explanation.

Consistent, fast, and every reply is itself a demonstration of the thesis.

The vertical cut is always the reveal

Never the build. Nobody wants three minutes of waiting on a phone.

Clip the moment the thing works, the moment the kid wins, the moment the date confirms. One per episode, cut the day you shoot.

Under two hundred dollars of gear

One camera, one lavalier, natural light, and a phone on a cheap overhead arm for the desk shots.

If the show looks expensive, the argument gets weaker. Kitchen-table production is the credibility.

Two episodes per Saturday

Five Saturday mornings, batch-shot, same shirt, same light. Edit on weeknights an hour at a time.

The season is done before the first one publishes. Then it releases weekly for ten weeks while you do nothing.

Ten and stop

Season one is a season, not a subscription. Publish ten, then go quiet and read the numbers.

A finished ten-episode season is a body of work. Twenty-two episodes that trail off is a graveyard, and everybody can see it in the upload dates.

Note from SodClaw

The batch-shoot isn't a production preference. It's the 80% guard.

Your pattern is well documented and it will absolutely come for this: episodes one through five will be a joy, episode six will be fine, and episodes seven through ten are exactly where a project like this normally dies — half-shot, in a folder, permanently "almost ready."

So the release schedule can't depend on future you. Shoot all ten before publishing any. Five Saturdays, two per morning, one shirt. If the whole season is in the can before episode one goes out, the last 20% is just editing — and editing on a deadline you've already announced is the one kind of finishing work you reliably do.

The other guard is scope: the fourteen bench ideas above are logged, not started. None of them gets a folder, a file, or a Saturday until episode ten is published. If you find yourself excited about "Translate Grandpa" in week three, that's the 80% wall arriving early wearing a costume.
